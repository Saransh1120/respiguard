What this shows

The waveform comes from the simulation. The spectrum and spectrogram are not drawn by hand. They are computed from the waveform with a 256-point FFT at 4 kHz, the same settings our training script uses.

A wheeze shows up as a thin line that stays at one frequency. Normal breath noise is spread out along the bottom. Pick Possible wheeze above and a line appears near 350 Hz.

Why 4 kHz

Most wheeze energy is between 100 Hz and 1 kHz. The chest wall filters out most sound above 1 kHz before it reaches the skin. Sampling at 4 kHz records everything up to 2 kHz, which covers that range.

The classifier was trained on 4 kHz audio, so the device has to record at 4 kHz as well. If the rates do not match, the model's output means nothing.

Scoring

Score terms same as the firmware

ConditionPoints
Wheeze probability above 0.50+2
SpO2 more than 3 points below baseline+2
Breathing rate more than 20% above baseline+1
Heart rate more than 15% above baseline+1
Moving (activity above 0.35) with no wheeze−2

The total decides the state. 0 or 1 is Normal, 2 or 3 is Watch, 4 or more is Alert. A higher state shows straight away. A lower state only shows after the current one has lasted 4 seconds, the same as the firmware, so the screen does not flicker.

Why movement takes points off

Climbing stairs raises breathing rate and heart rate and can lower SpO2 a little. Without the −2 that would look like an episode. Try Elevated activity above: breathing and heart rate each add 1, movement takes 2 off, and the score stays at 0.

Breathing rate and heart rate are worth 1 point each, so on their own they cannot reach Alert.

Environment

Temperature and humidity are not part of the score. They are saved with each event.

We have not tested yet whether combining these sensors works better than one sensor alone. That is what we want to measure once the hardware is built.

Pipeline

RespiGuard is a chest-worn device we are building for SIH26181. It records breath sound and reads SpO2, heart rate and breathing rate at the chest. The ESP32-S3 turns these into features, runs a small wheeze classifier, and compares everything with the wearer's own baseline.

Why a personal baseline

Normal resting SpO2 is 99% for some people and 95% for others. One fixed limit would be wrong for one of them. So the device first learns each wearer's normal values and then scores changes from those.

What it does not do

It gives a wheeze probability and a risk level. It does not diagnose anything and does not suggest treatment. On an alert it tells the wearer to stop, rest and follow the plan their doctor gave them.

Accuracy

We do not have accuracy numbers of our own yet. The classifier has not been tested on our hardware.

Switching to real hardware

The simulation produces one set of values per frame: SpO2, heart rate, breathing rate, wheeze probability, activity, temperature, humidity, score and state. Every panel reads only those values. When the device is ready it will provide the same values, and the panels stay as they are.

RespiGuard is a student prototype, not a medical device. The readings on this site are simulated and must not be used for medical decisions.
