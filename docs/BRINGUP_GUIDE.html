Workbench procedure

Bringing RespiGuard up, one part at a time

Thirty-five components, six breakout boards and a battery, assembled by hand. The order below is not a preference — it is the order in which a mistake stays cheap. Every step ends with something you must see before the next one begins.

Board ESP32-S3-DevKitC-1 Self-test RespiGuard.ino Serial 115200 baud

The rule this whole procedure rests on

Add one part. Test it. Only then add the next. Soldering everything and powering up once is how a build reaches a state where nothing works and there are six possible reasons why. Every fault you meet here should have exactly one candidate cause: the thing you just attached.

Phase 0

Before the iron is even hot

Two of the parts that arrive can be the wrong part while carrying the right name. Both are cheaper to catch now than after they are soldered down.

01

Count the chips on the TP4056

Look at the charger board under a light. You need three black chips: the TP4056 itself, a DW01 and an FS8205. Boards with only the TP4056 are sold under the same name and the same photo.

Reject A single-chip board has no over-discharge protection. It will run the Li-Po flat past 2.5 V and permanently kill the cell — quietly, over one long night.
02

Confirm the ESP32 board has PSRAM

The wheeze classifier asks for 234 KB of working memory at startup. A compile of the finished firmware leaves only 185 KB of internal RAM free, so that allocation has to land in PSRAM.

Look for N8R8 or N16R8 in the board's markings — the R8 is the PSRAM.

If absent The firmware still runs, but classifier.begin() returns false and the serial banner prints model FAILED. Wheeze detection is then dead and the score loses its heaviest term.
Phase 1

Power, with nothing else connected

Power comes first and alone. A rail set wrong with the ESP32 already attached destroys the most expensive part on the bench in the time it takes to notice.

03

Set the buck-boost to 3.3 V before it touches anything

Feed the module from the battery or a USB supply. Put the multimeter on its output, turn the trimmer, and bring it to 3.30 V. Nothing else is wired at this point.

Confirm Multimeter reads between 3.25 and 3.35 V, steady, with the supply moving.
Why alone These modules ship set anywhere from 1 V to 12 V. Wiring first and adjusting after puts whatever it happened to be set to straight across a 3.3 V part.
04

Battery, switch and charger

Li-Po into the TP4056 through the JST connector. The SPDT slide switch goes between the charger's output and the buck-boost input, so it cuts the battery and not the charging path.

Confirm Switch on gives 3.3 V at the buck-boost output; switch off gives 0 V. Plugging USB into the TP4056 lights its charge LED with the switch in either position.
05

Decoupling capacitors

The 470 µF electrolytic goes across the 3.3 V rail close to the buck-boost — mind the polarity stripe. A 100 nF ceramic goes next to each of the four breakout boards' power pins.

Confirm Rail still reads 3.3 V, and continuity between the 470 µF terminals reads open, not a short.
Phase 2

The ESP32 alone

Before any sensor, prove the board itself and the one analogue circuit that is easy to get wrong.

06

Blink, over USB only

Board still off the battery rail. Flash anything that toggles a pin and confirm the Arduino IDE can talk to it.

Confirm Sketch uploads, and the serial monitor shows output at 115200.
07

Battery divider on GPIO 1

Two 100 kΩ resistors in series across the battery, with GPIO 1 at their junction. The firmware reads that pin and doubles it.

Confirm The voltage the firmware prints matches what the multimeter reads across the cell, within about 0.1 V.
Phase 3

I²C, one device per test

All four I²C parts share two wires — SDA 8 and SCL 9. Attach them one at a time and run RespiGuard.ino after each. It probes every address and prints a pass/fail table, and it needs no libraries.

08

OLED first, because it proves the bus

The display is the one I²C part whose success is visible without reading a number. If it draws, SDA, SCL, power and ground are all correct, and every later failure belongs to the part being added rather than to the wiring underneath it.

Confirm Self-test reports 0x3C found, and something appears on the screen.
09

MAX30102

Register 0xFF must return 0x15. With a finger on it, the red LED should be faintly visible through the skin.

Confirm 0x57 answers and the part ID reads 0x15.
10

BME680 — and the substitution to watch for

Register 0xD0 must return 0x61.

If 0x58 You have a BMP280 sold as a BME680. It is a common substitution. Pressure and temperature will work; the gas sensor does not exist on that die. Return it.
11

BMI270

Register 0x00 must return 0x24. This part needs an 8 KB configuration blob uploaded at every power-on before it produces data, which is why the firmware drives it through the SparkFun library rather than raw I²C like the others.

Confirm ID reads 0x24, and tilting the board changes the acceleration values.
Phase 4

The microphone

The INMP441 is on I²S, not I²C, so nothing in the self-test table covers it. It is also the part the whole wheeze track depends on.

12

INMP441, with L/R tied to ground

Wire BCLK 4, WS 5, SD 6. The board's L/R pin must go to ground: the firmware opens the port as ONLY_LEFT, and a mic left on the right channel returns a buffer of zeros with no error anywhere.

Confirm Print the raw I²S samples. Silence should still show small non-zero values that jump when you speak. A perfectly flat zero means the channel is wrong, not that the room is quiet.
13

Mount it facing the chest, on the base

Breath sounds travel better through the body than through air. The mic port goes on the underside of the enclosure, against the skin — not on the top edge where it would mostly hear the room.

Phase 5

Outputs and the button

The cheapest parts, and the last, because none of them can hide a fault in anything else.

14

LEDs

Green on 15, red on 16, each through a 220 Ω resistor to ground.

15

Vibration motor — the diode is not optional

GPIO 17 through 1 kΩ into the base of the 2N2222; motor between the collector and 3.3 V; emitter to ground.

Flyback The 1N4148 goes across the motor terminals, banded end to the positive side. Without it, every switch-off sends an inductive spike back into the transistor and onto the rail — which shows up later as an ESP32 that resets at random and looks like a firmware bug.
16

Button on GPIO 18

One side to the pin, the other to ground. No pull-up resistor — the pin is configured INPUT_PULLUP in firmware.

Confirm Short press marks an event and buzzes once; holding 1.5 s clears the baseline and restarts learning.

Phase 7

Teaching the classifier what your hardware sounds like

The model was trained on ICBHI — a chest-contact stethoscope. Your microphone sits under clothing. That gap is the largest single risk in the project, and this phase is what closes it.

18

Record 50–100 normal breaths on the finished device

Worn as it will actually be worn: strap on, clothing over it, sitting and walking. Normal breathing only, which raises no ethics question because it is the negative class.

Not yet written This needs a recording sketch that streams raw I²S to serial — it does not exist yet. It is small, and it can be written before the parts arrive.
19

Fine-tune, re-export, re-verify

Continue training from the ICBHI weights with those recordings mixed in, then run export_weights.py and verify_export.py. The second one is not a formality: it is what proves the header the firmware compiles in computes the same thing the network you just measured computes.

Confirm verify_export.py prints PASS, and the firmware still compiles with the new weights.
20

Wear it for three days

The baseline window is three days on hardware, not the twenty seconds the simulation used. Until that window closes the device shows LEARNING and will not raise a state — it has no personal baseline to deviate from yet.

What this procedure does not give you

Finishing every step above gets you a device that detects wheeze on your own hardware and scores it against your own baseline. It does not get you a validated medical instrument, and the difference matters when describing the project.

Validation against real patients runs through a clinician, with written consent, and is a separate piece of work. Until that happens it belongs in any write-up as future work — never as something already done.
